OEM vs ODM For Luggage Brands

Introduction

Practical notes from DJI Luggage on building reliable suitcase and travel product programs, from early product planning to repeatable production.

Date10.02.25
AuthorDJI Luggage Team
TypeInsights

OEM or ODM: the short answer

There are two ways to have luggage made for your brand, and the choice decides who owns the design, who pays for tooling, and how long it takes to reach a first shipment.

In an OEM program you bring the specification. The factory builds to your drawings: your shell shape, your components, your interior, your branding and your packaging. You own the result, and you normally pay for the mould.

In an ODM program you start from a structure the factory has already developed and adapt it. Shell material, colour, hardware, lining, logo and carton change; the mould and the core construction are shared with other buyers.

OEM buys differentiation and costs time and tooling. ODM buys speed and a lower entry cost, and accepts that the underlying structure is not exclusive to you. DJI Luggage runs both models in Bogor, West Java, Indonesia, with a 200-unit minimum order, a 25–55 day lead time and 100% in-house mould making.

What each model asks of you

The label matters less than what the model requires from your side. Most suppliers offer both, so the useful question is not which model is better but which one fits the product you are trying to build.

Where the real cost sits

Buyers often compare OEM and ODM on unit price alone and miss the items that decide the total programme cost.

  • Tooling. A new shell shape means a new mould. Making moulds in-house removes the third-party queue, but the mould is still a real cost.
  • Sampling rounds. Each round consumes material, labour and shipping. A structure that already exists removes most of them.
  • Component minimums. Wheels, telescopic handles, locks and zips carry their own minimums from their manufacturers, and those minimums apply to both models.
  • Packaging and carton tooling. Nested sets, printed cartons and inserts carry tooling of their own, whichever model you choose.
  • Time. A longer programme ties up working capital and delays revenue.

On a repeat programme the tooling cost is spread across orders and the gap between the two models narrows. On a first order it is at its widest.

MOQ, lead time and payment

Both models run under the same commercial terms at our factory, so the model you choose does not change the minimum order or the payment structure.

  • Minimum order: 200 units per specification.
  • Lead time: 25–55 days from deposit and approved sample.
  • Payment: 30% deposit and 70% balance, unified for all order types.
  • Sample development: 7–15 working days after artwork and component direction are approved.
  • Capacity: 30,000 units a month, about 70% of it hard shell, from two 1,500-tonne injection machines and 100% in-house mould making.
  • Annual shipments: about 80 × 40HQ containers to China, Indonesia, Australia and Germany.

Materials and structures you can specify

The model does not restrict the material. Both OEM and ODM programmes run across the same range.

  • Polycarbonate and ABS+PC hard shells, moulded on site.
  • PP shells for lighter, value-focused ranges.
  • Aluminium frame construction: a PC or ABS+PC shell with an extruded aluminium profile frame, bent and riveted closed rather than welded. The aluminium is the structural frame, not the whole case.
  • Fabric and soft-shell constructions for ranges where packability matters more than rigidity.

Zipper and zipperless closures are both available. Component choice — wheels, handles, locks, lining — is quoted against your brief, and the same component minimums apply either way.

What we test, and to which standard

Testing evidence is the part of a supplier review that buyers most often take on trust. Ours can be checked against the standard.

  • Drop test: the case is conditioned at 18–25 °C for at least an hour, then released from 900 mm onto a 45-grade steel plate, once handle-up and once side-handle-up, to QB/T 2155-2018.
  • Low-temperature drop: conditioned at −12 °C for four hours, then dropped from 90 cm — five times on the base, once on each other face, and once on each of the four side edges.
  • Wheel test: a two-wheel case runs 8 km on a cement drum and then 4 km on a conveyor with raised cleats, with wheel wear staying within 2 mm. Cases also run down 25 consecutive steps at 200 mm step height under load.
  • Stacking: 40 kg on cases from 535 to 660 mm and 60 kg on cases from 685 to 835 mm, pressed for four continuous hours.
  • Salt spray: 16 hours to QB/T 3826.
  • Aluminium frame: the case mouth is tested by Brinell hardness to GB/T 231.1 and must reach at least 40 HWB, as required by QB/T 2155-2018. Anodising is the standard finish, with an oxide film of 10–12 micrometres.

Finished cases are sent to SGS for testing. Those are test reports on submitted samples, not a product certification, and component certifications belong to the component manufacturers.

What happens after you choose

Whichever model you pick, the programme runs through the same sequence, so you always know where your order sits. The full sequence is described on the production workflow page.

  • Brief and material direction. Size, shell direction, wheel system, handle, lining, lock, colour, branding method, target price and quantity.
  • Sample development. Seven to fifteen working days once artwork and component direction are approved.
  • Approved sample. This sample becomes the reference for bulk production and for inspection.
  • Bulk production. Twenty-five to fifty-five days from deposit and approved sample.
  • Inspection. Incoming material checks, in-line inspection and final packing review before goods move to export preparation.
  • Export packing and documentation. Carton marks, packing details and origin documentation for the destination market.

For OEM programmes, tooling and the first sampling rounds sit ahead of this sequence. For ODM programmes the sequence starts closer to sampling, because the structure is already proven in production.

How to choose between them

Pick OEM when the product itself is the asset: a shell shape, a structure or a component combination your customers will recognise, and that you intend to repeat across seasons.

Pick ODM when speed and cash matter more than exclusivity: a first range, a market test, or an addition to an existing line where the underlying structure is not the selling point.

Many brands do both. They enter with an ODM structure, learn which models sell, and move the winners to OEM once volume justifies tooling. That sequence keeps early capital low and spends on tooling only where the demand is already proven.

Questions buyers ask

Does ODM mean the product is not exclusive to me? The base structure may be shared with other buyers. Colour, hardware, lining, branding and packaging can still be exclusive to your programme.

Who owns the mould in an OEM programme? This is set in the commercial terms. Ask before the order, not after, because it decides what happens if you move production later.

Can I move from ODM to OEM later? Yes. Once a structure sells, tooling for a distinct shape can be quoted and the programme moves to OEM using the same components.

Does the model change the minimum order? No. Both run at 200 units per specification, with the same 25–55 day lead time and 30/70 payment terms.

How long does sampling take? Seven to fifteen working days after artwork and component direction are approved.
