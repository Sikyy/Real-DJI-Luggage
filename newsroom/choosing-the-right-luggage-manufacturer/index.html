What to verify before you place a luggage order

Evaluating a custom luggage manufacturer comes down to six things you can verify rather than trust: the shell material and case structure, whether the factory makes its own moulds, which test standards the cases are checked against and what the reports actually show, the minimum order quantity and lead time, the export packaging specification, and whether the factory can name the compliance rules its packaging satisfies. DJI Luggage is a luggage manufacturer in Bogor, West Java, Indonesia, producing hard-shell and soft-shell suitcases on an OEM and ODM basis. Two 1,500-tonne injection machines and 100% in-house mould making support a monthly capacity of 30,000 units, about 70% of it hard shell, shipping around 80 x 40HQ containers a year to China, Indonesia, Australia and Germany. The minimum order is 200 units per specification, and lead time is 25-55 days from deposit and approved sample.

Question to ask What a good answer looks like What to be wary of
Which shell materials do you mould? Named materials: PP, PC, ABS+PC, aluminum frame, fabric Marketing terms with no grade or wall thickness
Are your moulds made in-house? A stated proportion, plus machine count and tonnage A capability claim with no mould or machine detail
Which test standard do you work to? A named standard such as QB/T 2155-2018, with a report "International standards" with no number and no report

Shell material and case structure

Ask which shell material the factory actually moulds, because the material sets both unit cost and how the case behaves in transit. DJI Luggage produces PP, PC, ABS+PC, aluminum frame and fabric.

  • PP - the lightest hard-shell option, where weight matters more than finish.
  • PC - polycarbonate, which resists cracking across a wide temperature range.
  • ABS+PC - a blended shell balancing cost against impact resistance.
  • Aluminum frame - a PC or ABS+PC shell closed by an extruded aluminum frame, bent from profile and riveted rather than welded.
  • Fabric - soft-shell construction for programs needing expandability.

The frame is a structural component, not the whole body. If a supplier describes an entire case as aluminum but quotes a polycarbonate shell, the specification is inconsistent. Ask whether the frame is welded or riveted, and for the anodised film thickness. On the aluminum frame cases produced here the frame is riveted and the film is 10-12 microns.

Mould ownership and in-house capability

Moulds decide how quickly a new shape reaches production. When a factory outsources mould making, a new shell design waits in a third party's queue, and that delay lands in your lead time whether or not it appears in the quotation.

At DJI Luggage, mould making is 100% in-house and runs alongside two 1,500-tonne injection machines, which allows a new shell shape to be scheduled without an external tool shop. Three questions get to the truth:

  • How many injection machines does the factory run, and at what tonnage?
  • Are moulds made in-house or outsourced, and in what proportion?
  • Who owns the mould if you pay for a new shape?

Mould ownership causes the most trouble later, so the tooling agreement should say who holds it and when it can be moved. Our production workflow page shows how these stages fit together.

Test standards and the evidence behind them

Cases here are tested to QB/T 2155-2018, the Chinese light-industry standard the rigs are calibrated to. No equivalent ISO performance standard for luggage exists, so if your market or retailer requires a different protocol, the same tests can be run to it. Ask to see the report, not a summary. The case-level tests are:

  • Drop. Conditioned at 18-25°C for at least an hour, then released from 900 mm onto a 45-grade steel plate, handle-up and side-handle-up. Shell, case mouth and frame must not crack (QB/T 2155-2018).
  • Low-temperature drop. Conditioned at -12°C for four hours, then dropped from 90 cm: five times on the base, once on each other face, once on each side edge.
  • Wheel and step running. A two-wheel case runs 8 km on a cement drum then 4 km on a cleated conveyor, with wear within 2 mm; cases are also run down 25 consecutive steps at 200 mm under load.
  • Static load. Four continuous hours: 40 kg on cases from 535 to 660 mm, 60 kg on cases from 685 to 835 mm.
  • Salt spray. 16 hours, no more than three corrosion points each under one square millimetre (QB/T 3826).
  • Aluminum frame hardness. The case mouth is tested by Brinell hardness to GB/T 231.1 and must reach at least 40 HWB (QB/T 2155-2018).

When you read a report, check the conditioning step, and check whether it covers the finished case or only its parts. Wheels, handles, locks and zips are tested and certified by their component manufacturers, so the case-level report and the component certificates are separate documents. Finished cases are sent to SGS for testing; these are reports on submitted samples, not a product certification.

Minimum order, lead time and payment terms

Commercial terms show whether a factory is set up for the order size you actually have. The minimum order here is 200 units per specification, which matters most when testing a new market: a 200-unit run validates sell-through before you commit to a container. Lead time is 25-55 days from deposit and approved sample, and payment terms are 30% deposit and 70% balance, unified across all order types.

Sample development happens before that clock starts, so treat the sample approval date, not the enquiry date, as the beginning of your schedule. Two questions separate a realistic quotation from an optimistic one:

  • Does the minimum order apply per specification, per colour, or per order?
  • Does lead time start at deposit, at sample approval, or at material arrival, and which changes after approval push the date?

Export packaging and market compliance

Packaging is where a careful factory and a cheap one diverge most: the cost sits in details that are easy to omit, and the failures appear only after the container has crossed an ocean.

Export packing here covers single pack and nested pack, such as a 20" + 24" + 28" nested set; non-woven fabric or EPE foam between nested cases; a PE/PP dust bag on every case; die-cut corrugated board or EPE foam at the wheel housings and handle mounts; and silica gel desiccant or an anti-mould patch for container rain.

Packaging is produced to EU PPWR, US CONEG (heavy metals under 100 ppm) and REACH (no DMFu) requirements, and export documentation includes an Indonesian Certificate of Origin. A rule that is new to the factory can delay your first shipment, so ask which of your market's rules it already works to.

Warning signs in a factory quotation

Most problems with a first order are visible in the quotation before the deposit is paid.

  • A material named only in marketing terms. "Premium polycarbonate" is not a specification. Ask for the grade and the wall thickness.
  • Mould ownership left unstated. If you pay for tooling, the quotation should say who owns it.
  • Test claims without a report. "Tested to international standards" means nothing without a standard number and a report.
  • A day count with no start event, or vague packaging and capacity. "Export carton" says nothing about protection, and a capacity figure without units or a period cannot be checked.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an OEM and an ODM program? In an OEM program you supply the design and the factory builds to it. In an ODM program the factory brings product structures and component options and you adapt them.

Can I start with a small order? The minimum order is 200 units per specification, low enough to test a market before committing to a full container.

Do you claim a quality management certificate? No management-system certificate is claimed. Finished cases are sent to SGS for testing, and component suppliers certify their own parts.

How do I start? Send the size, material direction, target quantity and any artwork or reference sample through the contact page, and we will reply with minimum order, lead time and options.
