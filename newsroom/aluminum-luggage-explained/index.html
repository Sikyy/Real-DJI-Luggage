Aluminium Luggage Explained: Frame, Body And Finish

Introduction

Where the metal actually goes on an aluminium suitcase, the hardness figure to ask for, and how an anodised frame is tested.

Date9.26.26
AuthorDJI Luggage Team
TypeNews

Aluminium is a component, not a category

On a suitcase, aluminium turns up in four distinct places, and a case can use any combination of them: the frame around the case mouth, the corners and closure hardware, the telescopic handle tubes, and - rarely - the body itself. Which of the four a quotation actually includes is the difference between comparing like with like and comparing two different products.

That is why "aluminium luggage" means very different things at very different price points. It is a material choice, not a single product category.

What an aluminium frame case actually is

An aluminium frame case is not a solid aluminium body. It is a polycarbonate or ABS+PC shell joined to an extruded aluminium profile frame that runs around the opening. The aluminium carries the load at the case mouth - the point where a zippered case fails first - while the shell provides the body. The frame is bent from profile and riveted closed, not welded.

If a supplier describes a case as entirely aluminium while quoting a polycarbonate shell, the specification is inconsistent. Resolve it before you pay for a sample.

Frame hardness: the number to ask for

The aluminium case mouth is tested by Brinell hardness to GB/T 231.1 and must reach at least 40 HWB. That figure is a requirement of QB/T 2155-2018 itself, not an optional extra. A frame below it deforms at the opening long before the shell fails, which is why a frame quotation without a hardness figure is incomplete.

How the frame is made

The profile is extruded to the required cross-section, cut to the case perimeter, bent to the corner radii and riveted to the shell. The corners and the closure come from the same 6-series aluminium-magnesium alloy family as the profile. Anodising is the standard finish, with the oxide film specified at 10-12 micrometres.

Extrusion and anodising being outsourced is normal in this industry and not a weakness in itself. What matters is that mould making, bending, riveting and frame alignment stay inside the plant, because alignment is what the case mouth hardness ultimately depends on.

Testing that applies to aluminium frame cases

Structural testing follows QB/T 2155-2018. The aluminium-specific requirements are case mouth hardness and 16-hour salt spray to QB/T 3826, because an anodised frame is exposed to the same handling as the shell. Drop, low-temperature drop, wheel and stacking testing apply to the completed case regardless of shell material.

  • Case mouth hardness: Brinell, minimum 40 HWB to GB/T 231.1
  • Salt spray: 16 hours to QB/T 3826, no more than three corrosion points, each no larger than one square millimetre
  • Drop: conditioned at 18-25 °C, released from 900 mm onto 45-grade steel, once handle-up and once side-handle-up
  • Low-temperature drop: conditioned at -12 °C for four hours, then dropped from 90 cm
  • Wheel and trolley: 8 km on a cement drum plus 4 km on a cleated conveyor, wheel wear within 2 mm

One point many quotations get wrong: the roller-drum impact and falling-ball tests written for plastic shells are explicitly not applicable to metal-bodied cases. An aluminium case has to be tested against the aluminium requirements, not borrowed from the plastic test list.

Finished cases are sent to SGS for testing, which produces test reports on submitted samples rather than a product certification.

Aluminium or polycarbonate: how to decide

Most ranges carry both. A brand will put an aluminium frame line at the top of the range and polycarbonate or PP below it, because the frame signals the higher price point while the plastic shells carry the volume. The aluminium frame costs more, weighs more and reads as premium; polycarbonate is lighter and absorbs impact instead of transferring it.

Neither is better in the abstract. The question is which position in your range you are filling.
